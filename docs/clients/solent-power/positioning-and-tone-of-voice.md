## 1. Customer Profile

### Our Ideal Customer Is

The person responsible for keeping a site running when the mains fails, at an organisation where losing power costs money, safety or reputation within minutes.

- **Job titles:** Facilities Manager, Estates Manager, Head of Engineering, Operations Director, Site Services Manager, Compliance Manager, IT/Data Centre Manager, and the Finance Director who signs the contract.
- **Also:** M&E contractors, consulting engineers and main contractors who specify and procure standby power on projects.
- **Organisation types:** hospitals, private healthcare and care groups; data centres and comms rooms; food and pharmaceutical manufacturing; cold storage and logistics; water and utilities; hotels and leisure; universities and colleges; large agricultural operations; recycling and waste.
- **Size markers:** a standby requirement of 100kVA upwards, or several smaller sets across multiple sites. Typically 50+ staff on site, an existing maintenance budget, and an audit or regulator to satisfy (CQC, HTM 06-01, insurer, ISO, BCP).
- **Geography:** Hampshire and the South first (Southampton, Portsmouth, Winchester, Surrey, Sussex, Berkshire, Oxford, Gloucester), nationwide for contracts and projects.

### Our Ideal Customer Wants

A single critical power partner who designs the right system, installs it properly, keeps it serviced and tested, produces the paperwork, and answers the phone at 2am.

### What They Don't Want

- A supplier who sells the generator and disappears
- To find out during a real outage that the set does not take the load
- Four contractors to call (generator, UPS, fuel, electrical) and nobody accountable
- Paperwork they cannot hand to an auditor
- Being sold a set that is too big, too small, or the wrong fuel for the site
- Surprise costs after the quote: civils, cabling, ATS, fuel, permits, commissioning
- Engineers who cannot get there quickly, or who only know one brand
- To be treated like a walk-in retail customer

### Their Dream Outcome

Power is a solved problem. They have a partner who:

1. Knows the site, the load and the risk
2. Sized and installed the system to handle it
3. Services and tests it on a schedule they do not have to chase
4. Sends compliance-ready reports before the audit asks
5. Arrives fast when something does go wrong
6. Tells them the truth about cost, life expectancy and what to plan for

It should go without saying that quotes are prompt and prices are fixed where they can be.

## 2. Problem Analysis

### Real-world Problems

- Standby generators that have never been load-bank tested at full load
- Service contracts with a provider who only visits when chased
- Ageing sets with no replacement plan, and no budget line for one
- Fuel that has been sitting in the tank for years (contamination, diesel bug)
- UPS batteries past their life, so the generator start gap becomes an outage
- Generator, UPS, ATS and fuel handled by different companies, none of whom talk
- Expansion since the original install, so the set no longer covers the load
- Compliance gaps: HTM 06-01, BS EN 62040, insurer conditions, business continuity plans
- Slow or unreliable breakdown response, especially out of hours

Trigger question for: *"When did your generator last run on full load, and who has the report?"*

### Obstacles to Success

- Nobody in-house who understands generator sizing, fuel or controls
- Standby power is invisible until it fails, so it loses budget battles
- Procurement treats it as a commodity purchase, not a system
- Fear of disruption during changeover or installation
- Multiple sites with inconsistent kit and records
- Incumbent supplier is "fine" until the day it is not

### Emotional Impact

- Low-level anxiety every time the weather turns or the grid wobbles
- Fear of being the person blamed when the site goes dark
- Frustration at chasing suppliers for visits and reports
- Embarrassment at an audit finding they should have seen coming
- Relief and pride when the system just works during a real outage

### Major Frustrations (in the customer's words)

- "We bought the generator, and then we never heard from them again."
- "I have no idea if it would actually take the load."
- "I ring the number and I get a voicemail."
- "Every supplier tells me something different about what size we need."
- "The quote was for the generator. The install cost nearly as much again."
- "We've got three sites and three different companies looking after them."
- "I need a report I can put in front of the CQC inspector, not a scribbled job sheet."

# Solent Power Tone of Voice Guidelines

## Summary (one-liner)

Solent Power sounds like the senior engineer you want on the phone at 2am: calm, prepared, precise and plain-spoken. The voice is confident because it is backed by testing and paperwork, not because it shouts. Every line ties equipment to what it protects: the site, the patients, the production line, the reputation.

## 1. Voice Pillars

| Pillar | What it means | Example in this voice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Prepared | We plan for the failure so the customer never experiences it. | "Load tested every year. Proven before it's needed." |
| Engineering-led | Advice comes from measured load and reviewed specifications, not a catalogue. | "Every spec is checked by our Technical Manager before we order." |
| Responsive | 24/7, on site fast, one number to call. | "Engineers on call 24/7. Call 02381 550144." |
| Accountable | One partner for generator, UPS, fuel, testing and reporting. | "One contract. One contact. One set of records." |
| Straight-talking | Plain English, honest about cost and life expectancy, no scare tactics. | "Your set is undersized for the load you have now. Here is what that means." |

Tone keywords: calm, precise, prepared, direct, credible, dependable.

Reading age: 13 to 15 for headlines and intros. Technical detail is welcome further down the page, explained once.

## 2. Tone Dimensions

- **Formality:** mid to high. Professional and courteous, never stiff. We are writing to engineers and managers, not consumers.
- **Energy:** steady. No exclamation marks, no hype. Urgency comes from consequence, stated plainly.
- **Authority:** shown through specifics: kVA ranges, standards (HTM 06-01, BS EN 62040), test reports, response times, review scores.
- **Empathy:** we understand that standby power is a background worry with a foreground cost. We take the worry.
- **Perspective:** "you" for benefits and risks, "we" for what we do and promise. Never "they" about customers. Never "our passion".

## 3. Language Style Rules

**Do**

- Short sentences. One idea per sentence.
- Lead with what the site gets: uptime, compliance, a report, a fast engineer.
- Use the customer's own terms where they use them: kVA, standby, prime, ATS, UPS, PPM, load bank, HTM 06-01. Explain each once, in brackets or the next sentence.
- Numbers over adjectives: "24/7", "140+ installations", "4.9 from 118 reviews", "10kVA to 2500kVA", "63A to 2000A".
- Name the standard, the test and the document. "Compliance-ready load bank report" beats "peace of mind".
- Parallel structure for the service ladder: "Design. Install. Maintain. Test."
- Honest cost language: "fixed price", "whole-life cost", "what the install includes".

**Avoid**

- Retail phrasing: "for sale", "shop", "browse our range", "great prices" on commercial pages (size and product pages can keep "for sale" for search intent)
- Fear marketing: "catastrophe", "disaster", "don't get caught out"
- Passion and enthusiasm words: "passionate", "love", "thrilled", "amazing"
- Buzzwords: "solutions-driven", "best-in-class", "cutting-edge", "synergy"
- Vague promises: "unrivalled service", "total peace of mind", "zero downtime"
- Talking down: no "what is a generator?" explanations on commercial pages
- Exclamation marks

**Micro-phrases that fit:**
"Proven, not assumed." "One number, day or night." "Sized to your load." "Report in your inbox." "Tested before it's needed." "Any brand, any age." "Here is what that means for your site."

## 5. Sentence Mechanics

- Active voice: "We test the set on full load." (not "The set will be tested.")
- Front-load the benefit: "Stay open through an outage with a correctly sized standby set." Then the how.
- Trim filler: drop "very", "really", "extremely", "highly".
- Specific claim, then proof: "Engineers on call 24/7. Reviews mention on-site response in minutes."
- Consequence, not fear: "An untested set has a real chance of failing to start. A load test removes the doubt."
- One technical term per sentence, explained if the reader might not know it.

## 7. Do's and Don'ts

**Do**

- Put accreditations, the review score and the 24/7 number above the fold
- Lead with continuity and compliance, then cost
- Name kVA ranges and standards on every commercial page
- Use one short customer quote per page, with the customer's sector
- Keep CTAs consistent: "Book a free site survey", "Talk to an engineer", phone number
- Link every page to its neighbours in the service ladder (section 13)

**Don't**

- Open commercial pages with product grids or "for sale" copy
- Mix consumer and commercial content on the same page
- Use "passion", "love" or exclamation marks
- Promise "zero downtime" or "never fail"
- Bury the phone number
- Let a page end without a next step

## 14. Linking rules for every rewritten page

1. **Every commercial page links to its neighbours in the service ladder, in the body, not only in the nav.** Installation links to sizing, ATS, load bank testing, servicing and UPS. Servicing links to load bank testing, fuel testing, UPS servicing and thermographic imaging. Sales links to installation, used sets, hire and sizing.
2. **Two to five contextual links per page**, placed where the reader would naturally want the next page. Add a "Related services" block near the CTA with three links.
3. **Anchor text describes the target page**, using its main term or a natural variant: "generator load bank testing", "fixed-price generator servicing", "UPS installation and commissioning". Never "click here", "read more" or a bare URL. Vary phrasing across pages; do not repeat one exact anchor site-wide.
4. **One link per target per page.** The first mention gets the link.
5. **Sector pages link out to three primary services and one case study; primary services link to all five sector pages** in a "Who we work with" block.
6. **Location pages link to the five primary services with location-modified anchors** ("generator installation in Portsmouth"). Every primary page carries a "Service areas" block linking to the ten location pages.
7. **Every knowledge base article gets a commercial next step**: an in-body sentence linking to the relevant service, and a closing CTA block linking to the same service and the contact page. Examples below.
8. **Case studies and reviews are linked from every commercial page**, and each case study links to the services it used and its sector page.
9. **Size pages link up**: each kVA and kW page links to the sizing guide, installation, servicing and the sector that typically buys that size (for example 200kVA to 800kVA pages link to hospital and data centre pages).
10. **Brand pages (/brands/) link to servicing**, since the intent is "who services my FG Wilson". Servicing links back to a "brands we service" list.
11. **Fix URL variants before adding links.** Point every internal link at the canonical trailing-slash URL. Known duplicates: /generators/10kw and /generators/10kw/, /power-cut-checker and /power-cut-checker/, /Regions, /Standby. Redirect the variants.
12. **Power cut checker pages** get one standard block at the end: "Is your business ready for the next outage?" linking to /contingency-power-plan/, /generator-hire/ and /generator-service-maintenance/. Keep it to one block, so the pages stay useful to the public.
